# Five Economic Mechanisms — Cycle 10

## Mechanism 1 — Scope Economics
- **Economic logic:** orchestration authority and earned trust let the provider capture a
  larger share of the customer's total addressable services spend — moving from "one of
  many contractors" to "the primary integrator of most contractors." This is literally AMOR
  2020's own evidenced design logic (`EVIDENCE_REGISTER.md` A13).
- **Customer benefit:** fewer relationships to manage, single point of accountability,
  reduced internal coordination burden.
- **Provider benefit:** more revenue per account; more Pull-through surface area; fixed
  Control Tower/account-management cost amortized over a larger revenue base.
- **Required capability:** genuine delivery capability to run a much larger scope reliably —
  a real organizational stretch, not a sales claim.
- **Risk:** scope expansion can simply mean absorbing more low-margin, commodity
  subcontractor pass-through revenue without adding margin. More scope is not automatically
  better business — see Red Team item 1.
- **Evidence required:** whether real customers will actually consolidate more spend with
  one provider — untested; now the primary CFO validation question.
- **Kill condition:** customers use consolidation purely as procurement leverage while
  actually preferring multiple providers for competitive tension and risk diversification.

## Mechanism 2 — Duration Economics
- **Economic logic:** broader, provable accountability reduces the customer's perceived risk
  of a long commitment, supporting longer terms and/or higher renewal probability.
- **Customer benefit:** stability, reduced re-tendering burden, deeper cumulative asset
  knowledge from the provider over time.
- **Provider benefit:** Sales Cost and Transition Cost amortized over more years; reduced
  revenue volatility; more historical performance data supporting the attribution
  architecture (a virtuous cycle, if it holds).
- **Required capability:** sustained delivery consistency over many years — a single bad
  year can jeopardize a multi-year relationship's renewal.
- **Risk:** procurement policy or governance norms may force periodic rebidding regardless
  of performance.
- **Evidence required:** whether customers are willing/able to commit to genuinely longer
  terms versus policy-constrained shorter cycles.
- **Kill condition:** customers report hard procurement rules requiring rebid regardless of
  provider performance — this caps Duration Economics structurally, independent of anything
  the provider does.

## Mechanism 3 — Retention Economics
- **Economic logic:** deeper operational integration raises renewal probability, lowering
  expected Rebid Cost frequency and revenue-loss risk. **Must be earned, not structural.**
- **Customer benefit (only if earned):** superior performance, transparent governance,
  accumulated asset knowledge that genuinely makes the incumbent better than a new entrant,
  faster decision-making, lower coordination burden.
- **Provider benefit:** reduced churn, reduced Rebid Cost frequency, compounding knowledge
  advantage.
- **Required capability:** genuinely superior, provable performance — Event 4's track
  record and transparent KPI history, not the provider's word alone.
- **Risk:** the same mechanisms that create earned retention (deep data integration,
  accumulated institutional knowledge, custom processes) can slide into structural lock-in
  (proprietary formats, undocumented processes, punitive exit terms). See
  `EARNED_RETENTION_PROTOCOL.md` for the required distinction and customer protections.
- **Evidence required:** whether customers can articulate what would make them *want* to
  renew versus what would make them feel *trapped*.
- **Kill condition:** customers say they'd renew regardless of performance because switching
  is simply too costly — that is structural lock-in showing through, not a genuine economic
  win, and should be treated as a strategic and ethical problem, not celebrated.

## Mechanism 4 — Pull-through Economics
- **Economic logic:** the orchestration relationship generates visibility into legitimate
  additional demand (turnarounds, projects, reliability studies, digital, engineering) the
  provider is well-positioned to also deliver.
- **Customer benefit:** reduced friction sourcing additional work from a trusted party who
  already understands the asset base.
- **Provider benefit:** incremental, often higher-margin revenue beyond the base contract.
- **Required capability:** genuine technical/project delivery capability beyond maintenance
  execution — not guaranteed to exist in every provider organization.
- **Risk:** the agency problem — the same party that identifies a problem and recommends a
  solution also wants to sell and execute it. See `PULL_THROUGH_CONFLICT_PROTOCOL.md`.
- **Evidence required:** whether customers trust recommendations enough to award work
  without a competitive process, or would always require independent validation/tender
  regardless of relationship quality.
- **Kill condition:** customers state they would never award additional work without
  independent technical challenge or competitive tender, regardless of relationship — caps
  Pull-through's realistic upside to a lead-generation advantage, not a guaranteed-award one.

## Mechanism 5 — Margin Economics (efficiency, not price)
- **Economic logic:** operating a broader scope through one coordinated Control Tower may
  allow better resource planning, subcontractor leverage, standardization, and data-driven
  decisions that lower cost-to-serve — even at an unchanged customer price.
- **Customer benefit:** potentially lower total cost, showing up as competitive pricing
  rather than a payment event.
- **Provider benefit:** margin expansion from efficiency, not price increase — the most
  defensible, least controversial of the five mechanisms, since it requires the customer to
  accept nothing new commercially, only to observe better execution.
- **Required capability:** genuine planning/scheduling/procurement-leverage discipline at
  scale — operationally demanding, not free.
- **Risk:** running a broader scope could just as easily mean absorbing more complexity and
  coordination overhead that *erodes* margin, especially before the Control Tower's own
  operating cost is offset by scale. **Do not assume higher margins.**
- **Evidence required:** real cost-to-serve data across a broader scope — cannot be tested
  by interview alone; requires actual delivery experience or a detailed internal cost model
  outside this project's current scope.
- **Kill condition:** cost-to-serve analysis shows Control Tower coordination overhead
  exceeds any efficiency gains from scale — this mechanism would need to be abandoned as a
  value driver, though the other four could still stand independently.

## Cross-mechanism finding
The `ACCOUNT_LIFETIME_VALUE_MODEL.md` sensitivity test shows Mechanism 5 (Control Tower cost
control) is the single most sensitive variable in the entire thesis — more decisive than
Mechanisms 3 or 4. The thesis's biggest vulnerability is not whether customers renew more or
buy pull-through work; it is whether the Control Tower can be run efficiently at all.
