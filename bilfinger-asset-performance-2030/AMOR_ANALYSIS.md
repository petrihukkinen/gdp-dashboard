# AMOR Analysis — What the 2020 concept was, and what survives into 2030

Source: `01_SOURCE_MATERIAL/AMOR_DECK_TRANSCRIPT.md` (full extraction of the original 40-page deck). This document separates AMOR's business logic into what remains strong, what needs updating, and what should be discarded, then compares it against the proposed Bilfinger Asset Performance 2030 / APaaS concept.

## 1. What AMOR actually was, in one paragraph

AMOR was a **management buy-out and outsourcing structure**, not a performance-contracting product. Four Neste managers proposed spinning ~350 FTE of Neste's own maintenance/asset-management organisation into an independent company (AMOR Oy), which would then sell services back to Neste — first on a cost-plus/open-book basis (Years 1–2, markup factor 1.25), then on a fixed-price basis (Years 3–5), while also acting as the single integrator managing Neste's large subcontractor base. The financial case was framed as ~€43m cumulative savings to Neste over five years, achieved primarily through hands-on-tool-time improvement, subcontractor consolidation, right-sizing, and lifecycle/reliability work — explicitly **not** audited ("due diligence not performed," stated in the deck itself) and explicitly requiring joint effort ("collaboration between parties needed to reach potential").

## 2. Business logic extraction

- **Value creation mechanism:** cost takeout through (a) organisational restructuring/right-sizing, (b) reducing waiting time and increasing hands-on-tool time, (c) consolidating and re-negotiating a fragmented subcontractor base under a single integrator, (d) shifting commercial terms from hourly rates to lump-sum/fixed-price.
- **Risk-sharing mechanism:** almost none, in the modern sense. The commercial terms in Years 1–2 were cost-plus (AMOR bears no downside risk — markup guarantees margin on cost). In Years 3–5, AMOR bears fixed-price risk on the baseload only, with additional work, investment projects, and turnarounds carved out and invoiced separately. There is no gainshare, painshare, or KPI-linked fee — KPIs are used as **monitoring criteria**, not payment triggers.
- **Trust mechanism:** deep — an actual 350-person transfer of employment, a 5-year contractual lock-in, and founder-owners staying on as salaried employees. This is structurally a much deeper trust bet than a services contract; it resembles an internal carve-out more than a vendor relationship.
- **Governance mechanism:** a steering group, an annual-plan cascade from Neste strategy → 3-year KPI targets → AMOR annual action plan, and an explicit list of 11 working groups needed just to launch. This is thorough operational governance design, largely still applicable to any complex outsourcing.

## 3. Categorization: strong / needs updating / discard

### A. Ideas that remain strong (carry forward largely as-is)

1. **Single integrator managing a fragmented subcontractor base.** Reducing supplier count and creating one accountable throat-to-choke for delivery is still a first-order value lever in any complex industrial site — this logic transfers directly to Bilfinger's proposition.
2. **Staged commercial progression (open-book → fixed price → performance-linked).** The idea that trust and cost baselines have to be established before a provider can safely take on fixed or at-risk pricing is sound and directly informs the Phase 0–4 architecture in `04_COMMERCIAL_MODEL/`.
3. **Explicit KPI set tied to industrial reality** (operational availability, MTBR, TRIF, PSER, total maintenance cost) rather than generic SLAs. These are the right categories of metric; what's missing is payment-linkage and attribution, not the metrics themselves.
4. **Annual-plan cascade from customer strategy to provider action plan.** OPP/Neste strategy → 3-year KPI targets → AMOR annual plan is a clean, reusable governance pattern for translating customer intent into provider commitments.
5. **"Hands-on-tool time" as a named, measurable lever**, distinct from headcount or hourly-rate arguments. This is a durable, credible, and Bilfinger-relevant efficiency metric because it survives being audited on the shop floor.
6. **Explicit admission of unaudited figures and stated caveats** (p.24 of the deck: inflation, taxes, contract content, TA effects, "due diligence not performed"). This intellectual honesty is a model to reuse — Bilfinger's own board and any customer CFO will punish unqualified savings claims; AMOR's authors already knew this in 2020.
7. **Named change-management investment** (5–10 FTE change promoters) rather than assuming restructuring executes itself. Any Bilfinger transformation-services offer needs the same explicit line item.
8. **Long-term relationship structure with a small number of Main Service Providers**, rather than either full insourcing or a fully fragmented open market. This middle path (deep relationships with fewer partners, occasional-use tier for the rest) is a reusable supply-chain architecture.
9. **Long-term benchmarking against an external, credible reference** (Solomon benchmark quartile for refining) rather than only internal year-over-year comparison. External benchmarking gives a customer confidence the target isn't just negotiated downward each year.
10. **The core relationship principle — "mutual trust and constant endeavour for better results for both parties," under a committed multi-year term.** Vague as a sentence, but the operating implication (a long enough contract term that both sides invest in the relationship rather than defend positions annually) is a genuine enabler of performance-based contracting and worth preserving explicitly.

### B. Ideas that need updating for 2030 / Bilfinger

1. **Cost-plus-to-fixed-price is necessary but not sufficient.** It removes hourly-rate gaming but still ties Bilfinger's revenue to activity/cost, not to outcomes. Needs an added Phase 3–4 layer (KPI fee, gainshare) — see `04_COMMERCIAL_MODEL/`.
2. **KPIs need an attribution layer.** AMOR treated operational availability and PSER as if provider performance were the only driver. For a third-party service provider (Bilfinger) rather than an internal carve-out, this doesn't survive contract negotiation — see `05_KPI_ATTRIBUTION/` and Assumption/Risk notes below.
3. **The €1 business transfer / 350-FTE employment transfer is a Bilfinger-style outsourcing deal, not a services contract.** Bilfinger already runs its business as a third-party service provider; the AMOR model of *becoming* the customer's former internal department via an MBO is not how Bilfinger would enter new accounts. The reusable part is the operating model AMOR would have run, not the corporate-formation mechanics.
4. **Savings framed almost entirely as customer cost takeout.** For Bilfinger, the commercial model must also show how *Bilfinger* earns more, and earns better-quality (less cyclical, more recurring, higher-margin) revenue — not just how much cheaper the customer's maintenance gets. AMOR's deck is written entirely from the customer-savings point of view because it was an internal MBO pitch; Bilfinger's version must be symmetric.
5. **The 5-year lock-in with an internally aligned founding team (\"AMOR team, Contract team, HR team...\") assumed extraordinary internal access and trust** that a third-party bidder doesn't start with. The staged trust-building logic is right; the assumption of already having the relationship and internal information AMOR had (as literally the incumbent Neste team) needs to be replaced with a credible Phase 0 due-diligence gate for an external provider.
6. **Union/labour relations framing was Neste-Finland-specific** (rearranging union relations "in the service branch"). The underlying point — that a maintenance transformation is also a labour-relations and change-management undertaking — is still valid and scales internationally, but the specific mechanism doesn't.

### C. Ideas that should be discarded for the Bilfinger concept

1. **The MBO / spin-out corporate structure itself.** Bilfinger is already an external provider at scale; it does not need to "become" a customer's internal department to deliver an integrated model. Re-proposing an MBO-style transfer would confuse the concept and is not what Bilfinger's board would ask for.
2. **The €1 nominal transfer price and 350-FTE employment transfer mechanic.** Specific to a founder-led spin-out; not transferable, and legally/optically loaded in a way that adds no value to a Bilfinger pitch.
3. **Treating total plant KPIs (operational availability, PSER, TRIF) as directly payment-linked without attribution.** This is the single biggest thing to explicitly discard and correct — carrying it forward unmodified is the fastest way to lose credibility with a skeptical CFO or with Bilfinger's own contract/legal function. See CLAUDE.md rule #5.
4. **Single-country, single-customer framing (Neste, Kilpilahti/Naantali) as the end-state.** AMOR's growth path ("other customers in Kilpilahti," "satellite organisations at nearby industries") was appropriately modest for an MBO tied to one site, but is far too narrow as an ambition for a global Bilfinger initiative — it needs replacing with real multi-customer, multi-country scaling logic (see `WORKPLAN.md` and open question on scaling).

## 4. Comparison: AMOR 2020 vs. Asset Performance 2030

| Dimension | AMOR (2020) | Asset Performance 2030 (proposed) |
|---|---|---|
| Provider identity | Former customer department, spun out (MBO) | Existing global third-party industrial services group (Bilfinger) |
| Commercial arc | Cost-plus → fixed price | Fixed scope → KPI fee → gainshare/painshare, attribution-gated |
| Risk carried by provider | Fixed-price risk on baseload only (Yr 3-5) | Explicitly staged and capped; painshare only where attribution is objectively measurable |
| KPI role | Monitoring / relationship health | Payment-linked, but only for the attributable share |
| Trust foundation | Personal relationships + employment transfer | Contractual + Value Validation Board + staged track record |
| Scale ambition | One refinery, later "nearby industries" | Multi-customer, multi-country platform |
| Digital/data role | CMMS renewal as an operational project | Digital/AI as a scoped enabler of attribution and prediction, evaluated capability-by-capability |
| Governance | Steering group + 11 working groups | Value Validation Board + staged governance maturing with contract phase |

## 5. Ten strongest reusable principles from AMOR (ranked)

1. Act as a single accountable integrator over a fragmented supplier base.
2. Stage commercial risk — never start with fixed price or gainshare on Day 1.
3. Use named, industry-standard KPIs (availability, MTBR, TRIF, PSER) rather than generic SLAs.
4. Cascade customer strategy into provider targets into an annual execution plan.
5. Track "hands-on-tool time" as a credible, auditable efficiency lever.
6. State savings caveats and unaudited assumptions explicitly and up front.
7. Budget explicitly for change management as its own resourced workstream.
8. Prefer a small number of deep, long-term supplier relationships over either full fragmentation or full insourcing.
9. Benchmark against an external, industry-recognized reference, not just internal trend lines.
10. Anchor the commercial relationship in an explicit, multi-year mutual-benefit principle, not an annually renegotiated transactional stance.

## 6. Ten biggest weaknesses/risks in the new Asset Performance 2030 concept (first-cycle critical read)

These are the checker-pass findings against the concept as currently sketched in the user's brief — see `RISK_REGISTER.md` for the fuller register and `ASSUMPTIONS.md`/`OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` for what would resolve them.

1. **KPI attribution is asserted as solvable but has never been proven at scale.** Separating maintenance-caused losses from operations/feedstock/process/Capex losses in a live, adversarial commercial context (real money on the line) is materially harder than doing it internally at Neste for reporting purposes. No named methodology yet exists that a customer CFO and Bilfinger's legal function would both sign.
2. **Bilfinger has already publicly claimed this positioning** ("Your Performance Is Our Business," BCAP®). Pitching this as a new strategic direction risks looking uninformed about the company being joined; the credible pitch is narrower and harder — the commercial/contractual mechanism, not the ambition.
3. **Gainshare requires a trusted, jointly-agreed baseline — and baselines are gameable in both directions.** Customers fear an inflated baseline that pays Bilfinger for doing nothing; Bilfinger fears a deflated baseline it can never beat. AMOR's own deck admits it never did due diligence on its baseline; the new concept must show the mechanism that prevents this fight, not just assert a "Value Validation Board" will handle it.
4. **Customer adoption barrier: procurement and legal departments are not built to buy outcomes.** Most industrial procurement organisations are built around scope/rate-card RFPs; outcome-based commercial models often die in the vendor-approval and legal-risk-review stage regardless of technical merit.
5. **Balance sheet / financial-reporting risk of gainshare and painshare exposure.** Painshare commitments can create contingent liabilities that Bilfinger's CFO and auditors must reserve against or disclose; this is a real constraint on how much painshare Bilfinger can credibly accept, not a detail to wave at with "selected painshare."
6. **Scalability tension: attribution frameworks are plant-specific.** What counts as a "maintenance-caused loss" differs by process technology, asset age, and instrumentation quality. A framework proven at one refinery may not transfer cleanly to a different customer or industry, undermining the "scale across customers and countries" ambition.
7. **Data ownership and availability is unresolved and likely harder than assumed.** Attribution and AI-supported modules need process-control, production-planning, and reliability data that customers may treat as commercially sensitive or simply may not have in usable form (see `07_DIGITAL_AI/` discipline requirement). Assuming data availability is the single most common way "digital enablement" pitches collapse in due diligence.
8. **Organizational resistance inside Bilfinger itself, not just at the customer.** A KPI-fee/gainshare model changes how regional P&L owners are measured, how sales is compensated, and how legal/risk sign off deals — this is an internal change-management problem at least as large as the customer-facing one, and the concept as briefed is customer-facing only.
9. **Talent and capability gap for a "Value Validation Board" and attribution governance.** This requires a credible, resourced, semi-independent joint governance function — headcount, skill, and authority that doesn't yet exist inside a maintenance-services organisation and isn't free to build or run.
10. **Pilot selection risk.** If the first pilot customer/site is chosen for enthusiasm rather than data quality and attribution feasibility, the pilot will either fail to produce a clean case study or will produce one that doesn't generalize — undermining the very scaling story the concept depends on.

## 7. Bottom line

AMOR's real, reusable contribution is **operating-model and governance discipline for running a complex, integrated maintenance relationship at staged commercial risk** — not a performance-contracting mechanism, which AMOR never actually built (it stopped at fixed-price). The Bilfinger 2030 concept's genuine, defensible innovation has to be the **attribution and gainshare mechanism** that AMOR explicitly lacked — and that mechanism is also, per the risk list above, the single hardest and least proven part of the whole concept. That tension is the center of gravity for the rest of this project.
